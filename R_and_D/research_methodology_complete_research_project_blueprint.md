### The Three Elements of an Intriguing Research
1. What makes a research question interesting?
	1. Three aspects of an interesting question
		1. Important: It helps to solve a [general] problem or advances our understanding
			1. Societal problem 
				1. In applied context (say) - Business is facing the problem at this moment
		2. Novel: It hasn't been answered before
			1. There may be questions that are similar to my question
				1. Need to know how my question is different
				2. What does the question adds beyond the questions that have already been answered?
		3. Timely: It is the logical next step
			1. After the research that has already been done
2. Example: Gender Equality (research topic)
	1. Importance: "Gender equality is associated with lower violence against women, general safety, and economic prosperity"
	2. Literature review: "Research has distinguished X1, X2, and X3 as potential causes of gender inequality. However, there are indications that X4 is also a major cause of gender inequality."
		1. Novel
	3. Research question: "Does X4 cause gender inequality?"
		1. Timely - connects well with research that has already been conducted
3. Example 2: Depression 
	1. Importance: "It is well-known that depression can severely impair people's well-being and ability to function."
	3. Literature review: "Research has shown that depression is not caused the way originally assumed. However, there is no validated treatement that fits our current understanding of depression."
	4. Research question: "Can treatment X reduce depression?"
		1. It could follow a novel and current understanding of depression
4. Example: Leadership
	1. Importance: "Effective leadership is one of the main predictors of employee satisfaction and business growth."
	2. Literature review: "Research has distinguished several leadership styles. Recently, leadership style X has been proposed and some argued that it may be particularly effective."
	3. Research question: "Is leadership style X associated with business growth?"
5. Key lessons
	1. An interesting research question is:
		1. Important (helps to solve societal problems or understand fundamental phenomena)
		2. Novel (fills a gap)
		3. Timely (logical next step)

### Which Methodology to Use? Qualitative vs Qualitative
1. Qualitative Research
	1. Data: Text, images, audio
	2. Data collection: unstructured, flexible, sometimes subjective
		1. Example: interview or survey with spontaneous open-ended questions
			1. No plan as to where the interview would go, but the interviewer just follows the flow of the interview. They ask questions in whatever way it makes sense (open ended)
	3. Usage: Great in unexplored research topics and topics that are hard to quantify
		1. It is often not clear where the research should go
			1. Example: What questions make the most sense to ask
		2. We see where the conversation leads us
		3. Disadvantage: 
			1. It can be subjective
			2. Results can be influenced by choices the researcher makes during the data collection
2. Quantitative Research
	1. Data: Numerical, labels (example: whether a person received a treatment or not)
	2. Data collection: structured, standardized, objective
		1. Example: interview or survey with pre-defined close-ended questions
			1. Ordered 
			2. Possible set of answers
				1. Responders can select pre-defined answers
	3. Usage: Great for precision and zooming into details [with relatively high objectivity]
		1. Popular for more explored research topics
3. Research tends to evolve from qualitative research to quantitative research
	1. As the topic gets more explored
4. Example:
	1. Unexplored culture
		1. Go to people, have conversation, and see what topics pop up
			1. Example: General questions:
				1. "Would you mind telling me a little bit about your everyday life?"
					1. "I work as a manager and my wife works as a waitress"
			2. After talking to a few people, we recognize that there seems to be a pattern
				1. Example: Men tend to have higher positions compared to women
					1. How to quantify it?
						1. Think of comparing the annual wages of men and women
							1. Qualitative approach to quantitative approach
	2. Doctor visit
		1. "How can I help you today?"
			1. "I feel week since a few days"
			2. "Maybe you have a feber. Let's measure your temperature"
				1. Doctor has moved from qualitative approach to a quantitative approach (zooms into the details)
5. Key lessons
	1. Qualitative research uses a more unstructured and flexible approach, which works well in unexplored research fields
	2. Quantitative research uses a more structured and standardized approach, which works well for zooming into details with high objectivity
	3. In well-researched fields, quantitative research tends to be more common
6. As a new researcher, we usually try to fill a gap in a research field, so we might be involved in quantitative research

## Section 3: Construct a Solid Research Design
### How Your Research Design Affects the Interpretation of Your Findings
1. Four aspects of a compelling answer
	1. No alternative explanations
	2. Accurate measurements 
	3. External validity
	4. Reliability
2. Focus: No alternative explanations
	1. How to verify the following?
	
	```
	Independent variable -----> Dependent variable
	```
	
	```
	Number of friends --?----> Depression
	```
	
		1. Methodology:
			1. Conduct a survey
				1. Ask the following questions:
					1. How many friends do you have?
					2. How depressed do you feel at this moment on a scale of 1-10?
		2. It is numerical
			1. Number of friends
			2. Depression on a scale of 1-10
		3. Statistical relationship:
			1. As number of friends goes up, depression goes down
			2. As number of friends goes down, depression goes up
			3. Example study:
				1. 0 friends -> 9/10 is the depression
				2. many friends -> 1/10 is the depression
				3. 4 friends -> 3/10 is the depression
				4. ...
					1. We see a trend
						1. It is a statistical relationship
			4. What can we conclude?
				1. Possible explanation:
					1. Number of friends has an influence on depression
					2. Depression influences the number of friends
						1. Maybe depressed people have trouble finding friends
					3. Third variable explanation:
						1. Some third variable has influence on both variables
							1. Example: Job pressure
								1. High job pressure may reduce the number of friends 
								2. High job pressure may make you depressed because it is stressful
						2. There may be no causal relationship between number of friends and being depressed
				2. Why does it matter?
					1. If number of friends influence depression, we can advice a depressed person to find more friends
					2. If depression influences the number of friends, helping a person find friends would not help the person
					3. If job pressure is causing both, we need to change the job pressure (reducing the pressure may work)
				3. We cannot observe causal relationship
					1. We can only observe the statistical relationships
3. Key lessons
	1. There are three main explanations for a statistical relationship 
		1. The IV influenced the DV
			1. **In an ideal research design, only this explanation is possible**
		2. The DV influenced the IV
		3. A third variable influenced the IV and DV
	2. In an ideal research design, only the first explanation is possible

### Inferring Causation - Avoid This Common Mistake!
1. We need to check whether the research design is ruling out all alternative explanations
2. Statistical relationship = if one variable varies, the other varies systematically with it
	1. If you find a statistical relationship, always consider the three possible causal explanations
3. Examples:
	1. The more people meditate, the happier they get
		1. IV: Frequency of meditation
			1. Higher ---> Higher
		2. DV: Happiness
			1. Possibilities:
				1. Frequency of meditation ---> Happiness
				2. Happiness ---> Frequency of meditation
				3. Self-care motivation --> Frequency of meditation, Happiness (other things might be making them do both)
	2. Education protects people from getting overweight
		1. IV: Years of education (numerical)
		2. DV: Body weight (numerical)
		3. Statistical relationship: more years of education, lesser the body weight
			1. Possibilities:
				1. Years of education ---> body weight
				2. Body weight ---> Years of education
					1. Maybe body weight is causing physical problems which may make it difficult to get educated
				3. Strictness of parents ---> years of education, body weight (more education, stricter dietary rules)
	3. Entrepreneurship makes people rich 
		1. IV: Occupation type
			1. Entrepreneur vs employee 
		2. DV: Wealth: Numerical
		3. Statistical relationship:
			1. Employees -> Lower average wealth
			2. Entrepreneurs -> Higher average wealth
				1. Possibilities:
					1. Occupation type ---> Wealth
					2. Wealth ---> Occupation type
						1. As people get more wealthy, they are less scared to start their own business (business failure doesn't affect them)
					3. Ambition ---> Occupation type, Wealth (ambition might drive starting a new business, ambition might motivate them to earn more)
						1. Maybe, wealth doesn't depend on occupation type
							1. Maybe, only ambition drives them to become wealthy
	4. Growing up in Germany makes people boring
		1. IV: Birthplace
			1. Germany vs others
		2. DV: Boringness (numerical)
		3. Statistical relationship:
			1. Higher ---> Higher
			2. Possibilities:
				1. Birth place ---> borigness
				2. Boringness ---> Birth place
					1. Not possible usually (can be ruled out)
				3. Third variable -x-> birth place (cannot influence birth place)
			3. We can conclude the following:
				1. Birth place ---> Boringness (influence)
4. Key lessons
	1. Statistical relationship = if one variable varies, the other varies systematically with it
	2. Always consider all three causal explanations for statistical relationships
	3. Sometimes you can rule out some explanations
		1. Only if we are sure
	4. If in doubt, do no rule them out

### Sneaky mistakes that happen when inferring cousation
1. Another possibility:
	1. Boring parents ---> Birth place, Boringness
		1. How boring your parents are
			1. Let us say, Germany is attractive to boring people
			2. Let us say, boring people tend to have boring children
				1. Then we may find that people who are born in Germany are disproportionately more boring compared to people who were born in other countries.
					1. Statistical relationship between birth place and boringness
						1. Is it appropriate to conclude that being born in Germany causes people to be boring?
							1. If it was the right explanation, then allocating a person to be born in Germany makes that person boring
								1. That's not the case
									1. The real reason is the parents - Even if they lived in another country
										1. Non-boring parents Germany might raise non-boring children
			3. Third variable explanation has two causal links
				1. Having boring parents influences birthplace
					1. Boring people are likely to move to Germany and have children in Germany
				2. Having boring parents makes people more boring
	2. Interpreting statistical relationships is tricky
		1. We cannot be 100% sure what the causal relationship is
			1. There could always be something we overlook
				1. Reason for peer review
					1. We can ask other researchers for feedback as often as we can
2. There is an effective way to rule out alternative explanations

### The Most Effective Way to Rule out Alternative Explanations
1. Most effective way:
	1. **Manipulate the independent variable (experiment)**
		1. Example: Sliders
			1. If there is a statistical relationship
				1. Wire from first to second
				2. Causal mechanism from second to first
				3. Master slider influencing both
			2. Move first slider
				1. If second slider moves, then there is a causal relationship going from first slider to the second slider
			3. Move second slider
				1. If first slider moves, then there is a causal relationship going from second slider to the first slider
					1. If not, no causal relationship
			4. Manipulate third variables and see if there is an influence on the two sliders
2. How does an experiment work?
	1. Manipulate the independent variable, if we see co-variation in the dependent variable, then we conclude that there is a causal relationship going from IV to DV.
	2. We keep the other causal relationships open
		1. DV to IV
		2. TV to IV and DV
3. Example:
	1. Frequency of meditation vs happiness
		1. Put our finger on frequency of meditation and check if happiness covaries
			1. If there is statistical relationship going from frequency of meditation to happiness, then IV influences DV
				1. How does a study look like?
					1. We might ask people to do as much meditation as we instruct them to
						1. person 1: 10 hours
						2. person 2: 2 hours
						3. person 3: 5 hours
						4. person 4: 1 hour
							1. We as an experimentor decide how much each person meditates
								1. If happiness covaries, then there is an influence
									1. Happiness doesn't influence frequency of meditation because we control frequency of meditation
									2. Third variable doesn't control either or both because we control the third variable (same environment)
	2. Number of friends ---> Depression
		1. Move IV and check depression
			1. Instruct people to have different number of friends
				1. Not feasible
					1. It could be un-ethical
					2. Solution: Tweek the question
						1. Time since last encounter of friend ---> Depression
							1. One half - invite with their friends
								1. If this affects depression, it has a statistical relationship on happiness
							2. Second half - don't invite with their friends
4. Key lessons
	1. The most effective way to rule out explanations 2 and 3 is an experiment
	2. Experiment = a study in which the IV was manipulated by the experimenter (through randomization)
	3. Not all research questions lend themselves for experiments... formulate your research question wisely!

### Types of Research Designs
1. Example: Treatment (no treatment) ---> Illness
	1. How to implement?
		1. Divide participants into
			1. Treatment group
				1. Treatment is given
			2. Control group
				1. No treatment
			3. We ask: Is illness lower in the treatment group compared to the control group?
		2. We do the treatment for all the participants
			1. We measure illness several times for each participant
				1. Measurement 1 of illness
				2. Treatment
				2. Measurement 2 of illness
				3. ...
			2. Question: Is illness lower after treatment compared to before treatment?
				1. Called: Within-subject design
		3. Use both
			1. Divide participants into two groups
			2. Measurement 1 of illness in both groups
			3. Apply treatment to one group
			4. Measurement 2 of illness in both groups
			5. ...
			6. Question: Did illness reduce more (measurement 2 vs 1) in the treatment group compared to the control group?
				1. Called Mixed design
2. Research Designs:
	1. Between-subject design: Split your participants into several groups representing the levels of your independent variable (e.g., treatment, no treatment)
	2. Within-subject design: Measure your dependent variable several times with different moments representing the levels of your independent variable (e.g., treatment, no treatment)
	3. Mixed design: Split your participants into several groups representing the levels of your independent variable (e.g., treatment vs no treatment) and measure your dependent variable both and after manipulating the independent variable.

### How to Avoid Unnecessary Complexity in Your Research Design
1. Example: 
	1. Treatment (yes/no) -> Illness (numerical)
		1. Another approach: Number of treatments (numerical) -> illness (numerical)
			1. Complications:
				1. Option 1: Divide group into many small sub-groups
				2. Option 2: Treat each participant as his/her own group
					1. participant 1: zero treatment group
					2. participant 2: 1 treatment group
					3. ...
			2. Second approach:
				1. Treat the group as single group
				2. Measure illness of each participant after a treatment
					1. Complicated
		2. Manipulating independent variable
			1. Move the slider from one extreme to another extreme (no treatment vs lots of treatment)
				1. Makes the effect more visible on the dependent variable
		3. Dependent variable
			1. Make it numerical
				1. For detecting supple effects
					1. We can see if illness gets better
	2. In an experimental design, it is usually best to have a categorical IV (strong manipulation) and a numerical DV (to be able to detect small effects)
2. Key lessons
	1. There are three main research designs
		1. Between-subject: compare people
		2. Within-subjects: compare time points
		3. Mixed: compare time points in different groups
	2. In experimental designs, having a categorical IV and numerical CV is often preferable
		1. Especially for beginners

### Why You Need to Randomize And How to Do It Correctly
1. Problem: We are biased
	1. You look like you gonna get better soon. I'll assign you to the treatment group
		1. The treatment group might get better because of the selection and not because of the treatment
			1. Could happen unconsciously
			2. Solution: Randomize
				1. Flip a coin and assign participants to treatment group
	2. Randomization: Ensures that the only systematic difference between groups/moments is your minipulation
		1. Other variables (age, motivation, fatigue, experience) may differ between groups/moments by coincidence
			1. We can deal with later with **inferential statistics**
				1. Random differences are okay
2. Randomization:
	1. Between-subject design: randomly assign people to groups
	2. Within-subject design: randomize the order of the conditions (often not possible)
		1. Impossible: compare illness before and after treatment (order of the conditions cannot be randomized)
			1. Order is fixed
		2. Possible: compare productivity with hard chair to productivity with soft chair
			1. Head: participant starts with soft chair and then hard chair
			2. Tail: participant starts with hard chair and then soft chair
	3. Mixed design: randomly assign people to groups
3. Within-subject designs
	1. Randomization: randomly determine the order of the conditions for each participant
		1. Head: condition 1 and then condition 2
		2. Tail: condition 2 and then condition 1
	2. Counterbalancing: systematically balance the order of the conditions
		1. e.g., let participants with even participant numbers start with condition 1 and participatns with odd numbers start with condition 2
	3. Both have the consequence that the conditions will have no systematic difference (e.g., in the fatigue or training) other than the manipulation of the independent variable
4. Key lessons
	1. To avoid systematic biases, a proper experiment users randomization
		1. between-subject design: randomize who goes to which group
		2. within-subject design: randomize or counterbalance the order of the conditions (if possible)
		3. mixed design: randomize who goes to which group

### Experimental and Non-Experimental Designs - Which One Should You Choose?
1. Between-subject design
	1. Example: Treatment (yes/no) -> Illness
		1. In a between-subject experiment, the experimenter randomly assigns the participants to the groups
			1. Consequences
				1. Suppose we find a statistical relationship between treatment and illness
					1. Explanation 1: Treatment has an effect on illness
					2. Explanation 2: Illness has an effect on the treatment
						1. Not possible
							1. We have randomized who receives treatment (not just less ill people)
					3. Explanation 3: Self-care motivation (third variable)
						1. Not possible
							1. We have randomized who receives treatment
		2. In a between-subject non-experiment, the experimenter has no control over the group to which participants belong
3. Within-subject design
	1. Example: Treatment (yes/no) -> Illness
		1. Measurement 1 of illness before the treatment
		2. Measurement 2 of illness after the treatment
		3. In a within-subject experiment, the experimenter randomizes the order of the condition (treatment, no treatment)
			1. Not possible with this example
				1. Another example: meditation (yes/no) -> Happiness
					1. Meditation week
						1. First 50% of participants (chosen randomly) are in this week
					2. No meditation week
						1. Second 50% of participants (remaining) are in this week
					3. The participation is switched
					4. If we have found a statistical relationship
						1. Meditation has influence on happiness
						2. Happiness has influence on meditation
							1. Not possible
								1. Randomized participation
						3. Self-care motivation
							1. Not possible
								1. People cannot control when they can meditate
									1. Their mood or motivation has no influence
								2. Randomized participation
		4. In a within-subject non-experiment, the experimenter does not control the order of the conditions (treatment, no treatment)
4. Mixed design
	1. Example: Treatment (yes/no) -> Illness
		1. Two groups
			1. First group: Measurement 1, Applied treatment, Measurement 2
			2. Second group: Measurement 1, no treatment (control), Measurement 2
		2. In a mixed experiment, the experimenter randomly assigns the participants to the groups
			1. Treatment has influence on illness
				1. Only this works
			2. Illness has influence Treatment
				1. We control who goes to which group
			3. Self-care motiviation
				1. We control who goes to which group
		3. In a mixed non-experiment, the experimenter has no control over the group to which participants belong
5. Comparison
	1. Between-subject
		1. Requires many participants
		2. Some alternative explanations
		3. Easy to analyze results
	2. Within-subject
		1. Requires fewer participants
		2. Some alternative explanations
		3. Easy to analyze results
	3. Mixed
		1. Requires fewer participants
		2. Often strongest design when it comes to ruling out alternative explanations
			1. Just a rule of thumb
				1. It gives the most information comparatively to rule out alternative explanations
		3. Analyzing results is slightly more complicated
6. Key lessons
	1. Experimental design = the experimenter randomizes the independent variable (e.g., treatment vs no treatment)
		1. Between-subject: the experimenter randomizes group assignments
		2. Within-subject: the experimenter randomizes the order of the conditions
		3. Mixed: the experimenter randomizes group assignments
	2. Non-experimental design = the experimenter passively measures the independent variable (e.g., who received treatment and who did not) without controlling it
		1. Between-subject: the experimenter passively measures group membership without controlling it
		2. Within-subject: the experimenter passively measures the timing of the levels of the IV without controlling the timing
		3. Mixed: the experimenter passively measures both
